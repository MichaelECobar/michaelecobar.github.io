## Working Papers
**Healthy Skepticism? Medical Mistrust and Health Behaviors [Job Market Paper]**

Abstract: Trust in the medical system has eroded substantially in recent years, yet its role as a determinant of health and health care use remains understudied. In this paper, I estimate the effect of medical mistrust on health behaviors and sources of health information using NielsenIQ Consumer Panel data. First, I use a causal forest strategy to predict heterogeneous changes in medical mistrust, drawing on plausibly exogenous variation arising from the COVID-19 pandemic. Then, I show that individuals with larger predicted increases in mistrust reduced their use of physicians for both medical treatment and health information and increased their reliance on natural or alternative treatments and online health information. These findings represent the first causal evidence that medical mistrust shapes patient decision-making across a broad, demographically diverse sample, with effects that persist even after accounting for education, income, and race. This shift away from clinical decision makers and toward online information and non-traditional treatments increases patients’ exposure to misinformation and to care that lacks professional oversight. Because mistrust operates independently of conventional socioeconomic factors, policies aimed at improving health care access such as reducing costs or expanding insurance coverage, may fail to address the trust problem driving these shifts.

**Clearer Lungs, Clearer Minds? Analyzing the Effects of Tobacco 21 Laws on Youth Smoking Behavior and Mental Health**

Abstract: Both the tobacco industry and anti-tobacco advocates have promoted competing claims about nicotine’s
effects on mental health in their advertising, yet causal evidence supporting either position remains
limited. To address this gap, my paper provides estimates of the causal relationship between nicotine
use and mental health by exploiting exogenous variation in youth smoking arising from the change in the
minimum legal sales age for nicotine products in the US. Using BRFSS data and a stacked triple differences
strategy, I find that raising the minimum legal sales age from 18 to 21 reduced everyday smoking by
2.4 percentage points (39.5%) and decreased the number of poor mental health days in the past month
by 0.5 days (11.2%) among affected cohorts. Effects are substantially larger among individuals with the
highest predicted probability of smoking with everyday smoking falling by 7 percentage points (65.6%)
and poor mental health days declining by 1.56 days (31.8%). Together these results show that raising
the minimum legal sales age of nicotine products to 21 effectively reduced youth smoking and improved
youth mental health.

**Yearning to Breathe Free? Cigarette Taxes, Smoking Bans, and the Well-Being of Smokers** 
with Kevin Callison, Silda Nikaj, and Rujun Zhao

Abstract: The impacts of cigarette taxes and smoke-free air policies on individual well-being are not well understood. Using a novel measure of population exposure to smoking bans, we find that smoking bans improve the well-being of smokers while cigarette taxes have a mixed effect depending on one’s revealed preference for cessation. Our results contradict models of addiction that assume preferences are consistent over time and provide supportive evidence that, for some smokers, behaviors and preferences are misaligned. These findings have direct implications for the welfare effect of public policies aimed at curbing smoking.
